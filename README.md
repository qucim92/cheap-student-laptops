# Best Cheap Laptops for Students: What $200–$900 Actually Buys, and How to Pay Less Than the Sticker Price

The price of a "cheap" laptop went up this year, and not because manufacturers got greedier. Memory makers are selling RAM to AI data centers, and budget machines spend the largest share of their build cost on memory. One budget-laptop round-up noted that every single pick on its previous list had left the market, with configurations that shipped 16GB for $429 in 2024 now shipping 8GB at the same price [1]. PC makers say the same thing from the other side: expect to pay more this year than last [2].

So the useful question isn't "which laptop is cheapest." It's "which machine does my coursework actually need, and how do I get out of the checkout with the smallest possible dent." Both halves matter, and the second half is where most buying guides stop talking.

## First, find out what your program requires

Before comparing spec sheets, check your school's hardware page. Six of the top ten public universities in one survey tell incoming students to buy a Windows 11 laptop or MacBook less than two years old, and most ask for **16GB of RAM and a 512GB SSD at minimum** [3]. Engineering, design, and CS tracks usually want more.

Two warnings worth taking seriously:

- **Chromebooks are a gamble.** Three of the ten schools in that survey discouraged them for some or all majors, and one university only recommended them "as supplemental devices" [3]. Chromebooks also got more expensive relative to what they offer, which undercuts the main reason to buy one.
- **ARM-based Windows laptops can block software.** Two schools told students to avoid Snapdragon-based machines because some software, including AutoCAD, doesn't run natively [3]. Qualcomm's compatibility work is improving, but a machine that can't open your lab software is not a bargain at any price.

If your program's list is clean and your coursework lives in a browser, ignore both warnings and save the money.

## What each budget band realistically gets you

Roughly $200–$300 buys a browser machine. $500–$700 buys a machine that will last four years. Above $700 you're paying for a better screen or a dedicated GPU, which only matters if your major needs one.

A few picks that hold up under that framework:

**Around $200–$300: browser work only.** Wired's pick at this level is the $200 Asus Chromebook CX15, with the screen and touchpad as the trade-offs [4]. Newegg has the ASUS Chromebook C423NA at $298.98 with a 14-inch display and a 180-degree hinge, which is genuinely useful for sharing notes [5]. The honest limitation is software: if a professor requires a desktop application, ChromeOS blocks you.

**Around $300–$400: the cheapest real Windows option.** The Acer Aspire Go 15 has been listed at $397.90 in a 15.6-inch 1080p IPS config with Intel Core i3-N305 and 8GB of LPDDR5, with 10+ hours of light-use battery [1]. A different Aspire Go 15 configuration (AG15-32P-39R2) has been listed at $299 with a weaker Core 3 N355 chip and Windows in S Mode [6]. Same name, very different machines. Read the model number, not the marketing name.

**Around $500–$600: the four-year machine.** Newegg's sweet spot for general studies is 16GB of RAM, a 512GB SSD, and a modern eight-core chip, which the Acer Aspire Go 15 (Ryzen 7 5825U, 16GB, 512GB) hits at $599.99, with a 32GB version at $674.99 [5]. That extra $75 is worth considering, because thin laptops rarely let you add memory later.

**Around $600–$700: the screen and battery upgrade.** CNET lists the Acer Aspire 16 AI at $700 with a 16-inch 120Hz touchscreen, 16GB/512GB, and over 17 hours of tested battery life [7]. Its HP OmniBook 5 14 rival also sits around $700 with an OLED panel and class-leading battery [7]. Mashable rates the Aspire 16 AI as its top budget Windows pick, noting it outpaces every other sub-$900 laptop it has tested [8].

**The new budget Mac.** Apple's MacBook Neo is the most frequently recommended cheap student laptop this cycle. Outlets have quoted it anywhere from $599–$700 list, with student pricing knocking roughly $100 off, and one table listing a $499 student figure [3][7]. It comes with 8GB of unified memory and 256GB of storage, so if you keep everything local, the $800 512GB config is the one you actually want [3].

**Worth knowing before you buy:** Acer announced the Swift Air 14 at Computex 2026 — all-metal, $699, 8GB/512GB, 120Hz — with an August release date [8]. If your semester starts later, waiting a few weeks may be the smartest move on this list.

## Questions students keep asking

**Do laptops for school earn cashback?** Sometimes. It depends on the retailer and the product line, and electronics have the most exclusions of any category. Several retailers exclude Apple products, gaming machines, or components entirely. Check the merchant's terms before a four-figure order [9].

**Can I use my student discount and cashback together?** Generally yes, since student pricing lowers the price and cashback applies to what you pay. The risk case is education storefronts, which are excluded by name at Dell, HP, and Lenovo [13][14][15].

**Should I just wait?** If you can, yes. The memory crunch has pushed budget laptop pricing upward, and deal watchers advise buying when the price hits your target rather than waiting for one specific day. Back-to-school season, Black Friday, Prime Day, and long holiday weekends are when the deepest windows fall [8][16].

**Is cashback worth the extra step for a $300 Chromebook?** At 4%, that's $12. At 15% during a boosted Best Buy window, it's $45, which is a real chunk of a semester's software budget. The step takes about thirty seconds. Whether that's worth forming the habit is your call.

**How long until I can withdraw?** Tracked in about two days, confirmed in roughly 90, sometimes 120. Then it's withdrawable to a bank account or PayPal as cash, not store credit [9][10][13].

## The short version

Pick the machine your program actually requires, and don't let a cheap Chromebook or an ARM laptop become a problem in week three of a class that needs Windows software. Buy 16GB of RAM if you can afford it, because you can't add it later on most thin laptops. Then attack the price from three directions: student pricing where the retailer offers it, a sales-tax holiday if your state runs one during move-in season, and a cashback click-through before checkout.

One caution, because it's the easiest way to lose money on this: a code you paste from a random coupon site can void the cashback layer entirely. Use the codes the merchant's cashback page lists, clear your cart first, and don't let a browser extension hijack the session.
